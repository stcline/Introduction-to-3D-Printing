# EDD 3D Printing and Additive Manufacturing

Welcome to the 3D Printing and Additive Manufacturing unit.

In this unit, you will learn how engineers use 3D printing to turn digital designs into useful physical products. You will explore different types of additive manufacturing, learn how FDM printers work, investigate filament materials, and use Onshape to create parts designed specifically for 3D printing.

You will also examine the environmental impact of 3D printing. Engineers must consider more than whether a part can be printed. They must also consider material use, failed prints, energy, durability, repairability, and what happens to a product when it is no longer needed.

Throughout the unit, you will work as part of a small manufacturing team. You will practice using printers safely, managing filament, preparing print files, inspecting prints, testing designs, and documenting improvements.

The unit ends with a design challenge: you will create a useful multi-part assembly that includes at least two separately printed components that work together.

---

## Driving Question

> How can engineers use additive manufacturing to create useful products while balancing function, material performance, cost, safety, waste, and environmental impact?

---

## What You Will Learn

By the end of this unit, you should be able to:

- Explain what additive manufacturing is
- Identify several types of additive manufacturing
- Describe how FDM 3D printing works
- Safely use and inspect FDM printers
- Select appropriate materials for a printed product
- Manage filament in a humid environment
- Explain environmental tradeoffs connected to 3D printing
- Design parts in Onshape for successful FDM printing
- Prepare models for printing using slicer software
- Test, revise, and improve a printed design
- Create a useful multi-part 3D-printed assembly

## Final Design Challenge

Your final project will be a useful multi-part 3D-printed assembly.

Your design must:

- Solve a real problem for a user, classroom, home, robotics team, garden, electronics project, or other meaningful setting
- Include at least two separately printed parts
- Include a functional connection between parts
- Be designed in Onshape
- Use an appropriate material for the application
- Be printed using an approved slicer and printer setup
- Be tested against design criteria
- Include at least one evidence-based improvement or revision
- Include a short explanation of the environmental impact of your design choices

Examples of useful assemblies may include:

- A modular classroom organizer
- An adjustable phone or tablet stand
- A VEX robotics tool organizer
- A Raspberry Pi or camera mount
- A cable-management system
- A sensor enclosure with a removable cover
- A replacement or repair part for classroom equipment
- A reusable bottle carrier or bag clip
- A plant-monitoring enclosure
- An accessible grip, holder, or mounting accessory

## Responsible Printing

A successful print is not automatically a responsible print.

Before printing, ask:

1. Does this object solve a real problem?
2. Will it be used long enough to justify the material and energy required?
3. Can the design be made smaller, stronger, simpler, or easier to repair?
4. Can supports, failed prints, and unnecessary material be avoided?
5. Is the selected material appropriate for the job?
6. What will happen to the object when it is no longer needed?

> The goal is not to print more objects. The goal is to design better objects.
