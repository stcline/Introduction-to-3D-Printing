# Lesson 1: What Is Additive Manufacturing?

## Lesson at a glance

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Duration | 60 minutes |
| Lesson focus | Additive manufacturing overview; FDM in context |
| Student grouping | Individual warm-up; teams of 3–4 for process-selection activity |
| Primary activity | Match manufacturing processes to engineering applications |
| Deliverable | Process-selection chart and exit ticket |

## Learning targets

Students will be able to:

- Define additive manufacturing.
- Distinguish additive manufacturing from subtractive and formative manufacturing.
- Identify the major categories of additive manufacturing.
- Explain why FDM is useful in an engineering classroom.
- Select and justify an appropriate manufacturing process for a specific product or engineering need.

## Materials

- One successful FDM print and one failed FDM print
- Optional examples of machined, molded, cast, laser-cut, or commercially manufactured objects
- Student devices or engineering notebooks
- Additive-manufacturing process cards
- Application scenario cards
- Process-selection chart, printed or digital
- Projector and presentation slides
- Exit ticket, printed or digital

## Teacher preparation

1. Select a successful print that is clearly functional, such as a cable clip, bracket, container, sensor mount, or VEX-related part.
2. Select a failed print that visibly shows a problem, such as poor bed adhesion, spaghetti failure, layer shift, warping, or unsupported overhangs.
3. Prepare one set of process cards and application cards for each group.
4. Set up a visible class chart or slide with the three broad manufacturing approaches:
   - Additive
   - Subtractive
   - Formative
5. If possible, have one FDM printer operating during the lesson so students can observe the layer-by-layer process.

## Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Additive manufacturing | Manufacturing that builds an object by adding material in controlled layers or deposits |
| 3D printing | A commonly used term for additive-manufacturing technologies |
| Subtractive manufacturing | Manufacturing that removes material from a larger stock piece, such as milling, drilling, turning, or cutting |
| Formative manufacturing | Manufacturing that reshapes material through molding, casting, stamping, bending, or forming |
| FDM | Fused Deposition Modeling; a common name for filament-based printing |
| FFF | Fused Filament Fabrication; another name for filament-based extrusion printing |
| Material extrusion | The additive-manufacturing category that includes FDM and FFF |
| Build volume | The maximum size object a printer can produce |
| Prototype | An early version of a design used for testing and improvement |
| Post-processing | Work performed after printing, such as removing supports, sanding, curing, or painting |

## Lesson sequence

### 1. Entry task: How were these made? — 5 minutes

Display or distribute:

- One successful FDM print
- One failed FDM print
- One non-printed manufactured object, if available

Students respond independently in their engineering notebook:

> What evidence suggests that these objects were made using different manufacturing processes?

Students should identify at least two observable features.

Possible observations:

- Visible layers
- Smooth molded surfaces
- Material removed by cutting or drilling
- Support marks
- Rough or irregular failed-print surfaces
- Seams, tool marks, or injection-molding gates

Do not confirm or correct answers yet. The purpose is to reveal students’ current ideas about manufacturing.

### 2. Direct instruction: Three ways to make things — 10 minutes

Introduce the three broad categories of manufacturing.

| Manufacturing approach | What happens to material? | Common examples |
|---|---|---|
| Additive | Material is added to build a part | FDM, resin printing, metal powder-bed fusion |
| Subtractive | Material is removed from a larger stock piece | Milling, drilling, turning, CNC routing, laser cutting |
| Formative | Material is shaped or reshaped | Injection molding, casting, bending, stamping |

Use the following example to help students compare material use.

A rectangular stock block has dimensions:

\[
100 \text{ mm} \times 60 \text{ mm} \times 20 \text{ mm}
\]

The volume of the stock is:

\[
V = lwh
\]

\[
V = 100 \times 60 \times 20
\]

\[
V = 120{,}000 \text{ mm}^3
\]

If a subtractive process removes 75% of the original stock, the removed volume is:

\[
V_{\text{removed}} = 0.75 \times 120{,}000
\]

\[
V_{\text{removed}} = 90{,}000 \text{ mm}^3
\]

Ask students:

> Does this calculation prove that additive manufacturing is always the most sustainable choice?

Guide students toward the conclusion that it does not. Additive manufacturing can reduce material waste in some situations, but it can also require electricity, create failed prints and support waste, use packaged materials, and produce objects that may not be recyclable through normal local recycling systems.

### 3. Mini-lesson: The additive-manufacturing family — 12 minutes

Explain that FDM is one type of additive manufacturing, not a synonym for all 3D printing.

| Additive-manufacturing category | Basic process | Typical materials | Example applications |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material extrusion | Melted or softened material is deposited through a nozzle | PLA, PETG, TPU, nylon | Prototypes, brackets, enclosures, fixtures |
| Vat photopolymerization | Light cures liquid resin layer by layer | Photopolymer resin | Dental models, detailed miniatures, precision prototypes |
| Powder-bed fusion | Thermal energy fuses powder particles | Nylon powder, metal powder | Aerospace parts, medical devices, complex production parts |
| Material jetting | Small droplets of material are deposited and cured | Photopolymers, wax-like materials | High-detail models, full-color prototypes |
| Binder jetting | Binder joins particles of powder | Sand, metal, ceramic powder | Sand molds, metal parts after post-processing |
| Directed-energy deposition | Metal is deposited and fused by a high-energy source | Metal wire or powder | Large metal parts and repair operations |
| Sheet lamination | Sheets are bonded and cut into shape | Paper, polymer, metal sheets | Visual models and laminated products |

Emphasize these ideas:

- FDM is also commonly called FFF or material extrusion.
- Engineers choose processes based on requirements, not because one process is universally best.
- Important selection criteria include material, size, strength, detail, cost, speed, surface finish, safety, and number of parts needed.
- The FDM printers in the lab are especially valuable for rapid prototyping, functional fixtures, custom parts, and iterative engineering work.

### 4. Team activity: Match the process to the problem — 20 minutes

Place students in teams of 3–4.

Provide each team with:

- Additive-manufacturing process cards
- Application scenario cards
- A process-selection chart

For each application, teams identify:

1. The most appropriate manufacturing process.
2. Why the process fits the application.
3. One limitation, tradeoff, or reason another process might be considered.

#### Process-selection chart

| Application | Recommended process | Why it fits | Limitation or tradeoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flexible bumper for a competition robot | Material extrusion using TPU | Flexible material; low-cost; rapid redesign | Slow printing; flexible filament can be harder to print |

#### Suggested application cards

- A student needs a low-cost prototype for a phone stand that can be redesigned tomorrow.
- A dental office needs a highly detailed model for a custom dental appliance.
- An aerospace company needs a lightweight metal part with internal cooling channels.
- A manufacturer needs thousands of identical plastic bottle caps each day.
- A museum needs a detailed, full-color model of an artifact.
- A repair facility needs to rebuild a section of a large metal component.
- A VEX robotics team needs a flexible bumper or wire-management clip.
- A foundry needs a custom sand mold for a complex metal casting.
- A student team needs a quick-fit prototype of a Raspberry Pi camera mount.
- A company needs a large batch of identical storage-bin latches.

### 5. Debrief: Why FDM in our lab? — 8 minutes

Bring the class together and ask:

> Why do we use FDM printers in this engineering lab?

Create a two-column class chart.

| Why FDM works well in a school engineering lab | Limitations engineers must manage |
|---|---|
| Accessible equipment and materials | Visible layer lines |
| Rapid prototyping and iteration | Print time |
| Wide variety of thermoplastic filaments | Strength changes with orientation |
| Useful for custom parts and fixtures | Supports may be required |
| Students can observe the manufacturing process | Material can absorb moisture |
| Lower barrier to entry than many industrial processes | Failed prints create waste |

Reinforce that the goal of this unit is not simply to make objects. Students will learn to make informed engineering decisions about what to print, how to print it, which material to use, and whether printing is justified in the first place.

### 6. Exit ticket — 5 minutes

Students complete the following independently.

1. Define additive manufacturing in one sentence.
2. Explain one difference between additive and subtractive manufacturing.
3. Which process would you choose for a custom VEX wire-management clip that must be redesigned quickly? Explain your choice.
4. Identify one limitation of FDM printing.

## Assessment

### Formative evidence

- Entry-task notebook response
- Team process-selection chart
- Participation in debrief discussion
- Individual exit ticket

### Proficiency indicators

A student demonstrates proficiency when they can:

- Correctly identify FDM/FFF as material extrusion.
- Describe a meaningful difference between additive, subtractive, and formative manufacturing.
- Use engineering criteria to select a process for an application.
- Name a realistic strength and limitation of FDM printing.

## Teacher notes

- Students may assume that all 3D printing is “better for the environment.” Do not fully address that misconception today; record it publicly and explain that it will be investigated during the environmental-impact lesson.
- Students may select FDM for every scenario because it is familiar. Continue asking, “Would it work?” versus “Would it be the best production process?”
- For EDD students, push beyond simple matching. Require language such as material properties, production quantity, geometry, tolerances, surface finish, iteration speed, and cost.
- Save the completed team charts. Their initial reasoning can be revisited later when students select materials for their final multi-part assembly.
