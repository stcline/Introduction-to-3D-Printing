# Lesson 1: What Is Additive Manufacturing?

## Lesson at a glance

| Item | Details |
|---|---|
| Time | 60 minutes |
| Focus | Additive manufacturing overview; FDM in context |
| Work format | Individual warm-up and teams of 3–4 |
| Main activity | Match manufacturing processes to engineering applications |
| Turn in | Process-selection chart and exit ticket |

## Learning targets

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

- Define additive manufacturing.
- Distinguish additive manufacturing from subtractive and formative manufacturing.
- Identify the major categories of additive manufacturing.
- Explain why FDM is useful in an engineering classroom.
- Select and justify an appropriate manufacturing process for a specific product or engineering need.

## Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Additive manufacturing | Manufacturing that builds an object by adding material in controlled layers or deposits |
| 3D printing | A commonly used term for additive-manufacturing technologies |
| Subtractive manufacturing | Manufacturing that removes material from a larger stock piece, such as milling, drilling, turning, or cutting |
| Formative manufacturing | Manufacturing that reshapes material through molding, casting, stamping, bending, or forming |
| FDM | Fused Deposition Modeling; a common name for filament-based printing |
| FFF | Fused Filament Fabrication; another name for filament-based extrusion printing |
| Material extrusion | The additive-manufacturing category that includes FDM and FFF |
| Build volume | The maximum size object a printer can produce |
| Prototype | An early version of a design used for testing and improvement |
| Post-processing | Work performed after printing, such as removing supports, sanding, curing, or painting |

## Your task

### 1. Entry task: How were these made? — 5 minutes

Examine the objects your teacher provides. You may see:

- A successful FDM print
- A failed FDM print
- A non-printed manufactured object

In your engineering notebook, answer this question:

> What evidence suggests that these objects were made using different manufacturing processes?

Identify at least two observable features.

Possible observations include:

- Visible layers
- Smooth molded surfaces
- Material removed by cutting or drilling
- Support marks
- Rough or irregular failed-print surfaces
- Seams, tool marks, or injection-molding gates

### 2. Three ways to make things — 10 minutes

Engineers use several broad approaches to manufacture products.

| Manufacturing approach | What happens to material? | Common examples |
|---|---|---|
| Additive | Material is added to build a part | FDM, resin printing, metal powder-bed fusion |
| Subtractive | Material is removed from a larger stock piece | Milling, drilling, turning, CNC routing, laser cutting |
| Formative | Material is shaped or reshaped | Injection molding, casting, bending, stamping |

Consider a rectangular stock block with dimensions of 100 mm × 60 mm × 20 mm.

The volume of the block is found by multiplying length × width × height:

```text
Volume = length × width × height
Volume = 100 mm × 60 mm × 20 mm
Volume = 120,000 mm³
```

If a subtractive process removes 75% of the original stock, the removed volume is:

```text
Removed volume = 0.75 × 120,000 mm³
Removed volume = 90,000 mm³
```

Discuss this question with your group:

> Does this calculation prove that additive manufacturing is always the most sustainable choice?

Your answer should be **no**. Additive manufacturing can reduce material waste in some situations, but it can also require electricity, create failed prints and support waste, use packaged materials, and create objects that may not be recyclable through normal local recycling systems.

### 3. The additive-manufacturing family — 12 minutes

FDM is one type of additive manufacturing. It is not another name for every kind of 3D printing.

| Additive-manufacturing category | Basic process | Typical materials | Example applications |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material extrusion | Melted or softened material is deposited through a nozzle | PLA, PETG, TPU, nylon | Prototypes, brackets, enclosures, fixtures |
| Vat photopolymerization | Light cures liquid resin layer by layer | Photopolymer resin | Dental models, detailed miniatures, precision prototypes |
| Powder-bed fusion | Thermal energy fuses powder particles | Nylon powder, metal powder | Aerospace parts, medical devices, complex production parts |
| Material jetting | Small droplets of material are deposited and cured | Photopolymers, wax-like materials | High-detail models, full-color prototypes |
| Binder jetting | Binder joins particles of powder | Sand, metal, ceramic powder | Sand molds, metal parts after post-processing |
| Directed-energy deposition | Metal is deposited and fused by a high-energy source | Metal wire or powder | Large metal parts and repair operations |
| Sheet lamination | Sheets are bonded and cut into shape | Paper, polymer, metal sheets | Visual models and laminated products |

Keep these ideas in mind:

- FDM is also commonly called FFF or material extrusion.
- Engineers choose processes based on requirements, not because one process is always best.
- Important selection criteria include material, size, strength, detail, cost, speed, surface finish, safety, and number of parts needed.
- The FDM printers in this lab are especially useful for rapid prototyping, functional fixtures, custom parts, and iterative engineering work.

### 5. Discussion: Why FDM in our lab? — 8 minutes

Discuss this question with the class:

> Why do we use FDM printers in this engineering lab?

As your class discusses, add ideas to this chart.

| Why FDM works well in a school engineering lab | Limitations engineers must manage |
|---|---|
| Accessible equipment and materials | Visible layer lines |
| Rapid prototyping and iteration | Print time |
| Wide variety of thermoplastic filaments | Strength changes with orientation |
| Useful for custom parts and fixtures | Supports may be required |
| Students can observe the manufacturing process | Material can absorb moisture |
| Lower barrier to entry than many industrial processes | Failed prints create waste |

Remember: the goal of this unit is not simply to make objects. You will learn to make informed engineering decisions about what to print, how to print it, which material to use, and whether printing is justified in the first place.
